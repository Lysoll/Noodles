Section 1.1

Definition and Forms of Communication

Communication is the process of generating meaning by sending and receiving verbal and nonverbal symbols and signs, and it is always shaped by multiple contexts. The section sorts communication into five forms, based on how many people are involved and how the message travels.

The five forms, from smallest to largest

Intrapersonalyou → yourself

Talking to yourself through internal vocalization or reflective thinking. Triggered by an internal or external stimulus.

Interpersonalpeople who affect each other

Between people whose lives mutually influence one another. Builds, maintains and ends relationships.

Group3+ people, shared goal

Three or more people working toward a shared goal. Often task-focused; more intentional and formal than interpersonal.

Publicone → an audience

Sender-focused. One person is usually responsible for conveying information to an audience.

Massone → many, through media

Public communication sent to many people through print, electronic or digital media.

Tell them apart

Group vs. interpersonal: look for three or more people and a shared task. Public vs. mass: mass communication goes through a medium (print, TV, internet) to many people; public is a speaker in front of an audience.

Section 1.2

The Communication Process

This section breaks communication into parts (who sends, what is sent, how it travels, what gets in the way) and compares three models of how the process works. Each model adds something the one before it left out.

Three models of communication

ModelHow it sees communicationKey features
TransmissionLinear and one-way. A sender intentionally transmits a message to a receiver.Also called the injection model. No feedback.
InteractionParticipants take turns being sender and receiver.Adds feedback. Accounts for physical and psychological context.
TransactionalWe are senders and receivers at the same time, and communication builds our social realities.Happens within social, relational and cultural contexts.
Memory hook

Transmission = a one-way broadcast. Interaction = taking turns, like playing catch. Transactional = a live conversation where both people send and receive at once, and together shape what is "real."

Parts of the process

  • Participants send and receive messages (verbal or nonverbal content).
  • Encoding turns thoughts into communication; decoding turns communication back into thoughts.
  • Messages travel through a channel, usually visual and/or auditory.
  • Feedback is a message sent in response to another message.
  • Noise is anything that interferes. Environmental noise is physical sound in the setting; semantic noise happens when people don't understand a symbol or give it different meanings.

Five contexts

ContextWhat it coversModel
PhysicalSize, layout, temperature, lightingInteraction
PsychologicalStress, anxiety, emotionsInteraction
SocialStated rules or unstated normsTransactional
RelationalYour history and type of relationship with the personTransactional
CulturalIdentities such as race, gender, nationality, ethnicity, sexual orientation, class and abilityTransactional

Section 1.3

Communication Principles

This section explains why we communicate (to meet needs), how communication gets things done (compliance-gaining), and how it connects to ethics, civic life and social power.

Four needs communication meets

Physicalbody & mind

Keep our bodies and minds functioning.

Instrumentalgetting things done

Help us get things done day to day and reach short- and long-term goals. Compliance-gaining communication serves these needs.

Relationalsocial bonds

Help us maintain social bonds and relationships. Phatic communication (greetings, small talk) serves these needs.

Identityhow others see us

Present ourselves to others and be thought of in particular, desired ways.

Compliance-gaining tactics

Compliance-gaining communication tries to get people to do something or act a certain way. The book lists seven tactics:

  • Rewards: offer something in return
  • Punishment: threaten a consequence
  • Expertise: rely on your knowledge or authority
  • Liking: use the other person's affection for you
  • Debt: call in a favor they owe you
  • Altruism: appeal to their wish to help
  • Esteem: point out how the action will make them look or feel good (or bad)

Communication and social power

Field, capital, habitus and doxa are sociologist Pierre Bourdieu's terms. They fit together:

  • A field is a social setting with its own rules, knowledge and capital (what counts as valuable there).
  • Habitus is your feel for how to behave so you can move through that setting successfully.
  • Doxa is what "goes without saying" there.
  • Hegemony is when a ruling class shapes a diverse society's culture so its worldview becomes the accepted norm.
Tell them apart

Habitus is a person's sense of what's appropriate. Doxa is the shared belief that no one questions. Hegemony is domination by a ruling class that makes its worldview seem normal.

Section 2.1

The Perception Process

Perception is the process of selecting, organizing and interpreting information. It matters for communication because we respond differently to people and objects depending on how we perceive them.

Three steps, in order

1. Selectingwhat gets noticed

We focus attention on certain incoming sensory information and push the rest to the background. We select what is salient.

2. Organizingsorting into patterns

We sort and categorize what we noticed by proximity, similarity and difference.

3. Interpretingassigning meaning

We give experiences meaning using schemata. This step is often the most deliberate and conscious.

Selecting: three things make stimuli salient

Salience is the degree to which something attracts our attention in a particular context.

  • Visual and aural stimulation. Things that are visually or aurally stimulating get attention, in helpful or distracting ways. Speakers can use vocal variety (changing rate, volume and pitch) and gestures to hold attention. Nonverbal adaptors, nervous movements like pacing or twirling hair, distract.
  • Needs and interests. We notice what meets our needs (listening for your name in a waiting room) or interests us. Paying attention to interests that don't meet needs is how procrastination works.
  • Expectations. Both expected and unexpected things can be salient. If you expect a package, you notice the truck. An unexpected thing must cross a threshold of difference to be noticed: one extra student in class goes unnoticed, a classmate dressed as a wizard does not. Expectations also make us miss things, like typos when proofreading our own papers.
Organizing byRule of thumbBook example
ProximityThings close together go togetherA clerk assumes two strangers standing next to each other in line are together
SimilarityThings that look or act alike belong togetherA server assumes three friends with short hair and glasses are brothers
DifferenceThe thing that looks or acts different doesn't belongA Vietnamese American conference attendee is mistaken for a hotel worker

Also in this section

  • Punctuation is structuring information into a timeline to decide the cause (stimulus) and effect (response) of an interaction. Two people can punctuate the same conflict differently, which leads to misunderstanding.
  • Schemata work like databases of stored, related information, or like lenses. They also act as scripts for how to behave in places like a waiting room or a first date.
  • Being able to adapt your schemata is a sign of cognitive complexity, an important part of communication competence.
  • Grouping things is natural, but the judgments we attach to categories are learned. They become unethical when based on stereotypes or prejudice.
Proofreading tips from the book

Expectations make us skip our own typos. Have a friend proofread, or read your paper backward so you have to focus on each word.

Section 2.4

Improving Perception

This section gives two lists of strategies: one for improving how we see ourselves, and one for improving how we see others.

Improve self-perceptionImprove perception of others
Avoid reliance on rigid schemaDevelop empathetic listening skills
Be critical of socializing forcesBeware of stereotypes and prejudice
Beware of self-fulfilling propheciesPractice perception checking
Create and maintain supporting relationshipsEngage in self-reflection
Beware of distorted patterns of thinking and acting

Improving self-perception

  • Self-concept and self-esteem are fairly stable, but life-changing events (college, a new relationship, a child, a move, a new job) can change them quickly. Slower change comes from self-monitoring and reflection. Expect pushback from people who liked the old you, because people want predictability in relationships.
  • Rigid schema: familiar schemata become scripts and lead to mindless communication, like "highway hypnosis" on a familiar drive. Stay mindful of new information.
  • Socializing forces (family, friends, cultural norms, media) can push negative self-perceptions, such as unrealistic body standards. The fat acceptance and "Healthy at Every Size" movements push back. Members of groups facing discrimination often keep positive self-perceptions by identifying more closely with their in-group.
  • Self-fulfilling prophecies: a false belief triggers behavior that makes the belief come true. Self-affirmation (positive thoughts, focusing on strengths) can break the cycle.
  • Supportive relationships help, but surrounding yourself only with people who validate you can distort self-perception too.
  • Distorted patterns include overcompensating for low self-esteem (a midlife-crisis sports car, being a "big fish in a small pond"), setting unrealistic goals (goals should be challenging but progressive), and ignoring either negative or positive feedback.
Book example

American Idol auditions: contestants with distorted self-perceptions get biased feedback from family and friends. Setting incremental goals, like singing at a local event first, would have given them more accurate information.

Improving perception of others

  • Empathetic listening takes cognitive and emotional effort. "Everyone's biggest problem is his or her biggest problem." The Watermelon Fable makes the same point: to help people, first meet them inside their own perception.
  • Stereotypes are schemata taken too far. They can be positive, negative or neutral, and all can lower the quality of communication. Even "positive" ones like the model minority stereotype cause harm. Counter them by getting new information and basing impressions on describable behavior.
  • Prejudice is the negative feeling that results when stereotypes shape attitudes. It can harm one person or, when widespread, become social injustice (the book's example is the slow early response to AIDS).
  • Self-reflection means being aware of and able to recount your own thoughts and actions. It builds cultural self-awareness. Ask: "Is my impression based on how this person wants to be, or how I think this person should want to be?"

Perception checking, step by step

1. Describewhat you observed

Tell the person the behavior you're seeing, verbal and nonverbal.

2. Interprettwo possibilities

Offer two possible interpretations of the behavior.

3. Askfor clarification

Ask the person to explain. Then, as a best practice, restate your improved understanding.

Rules for perception checking

Keep your tone and nonverbals non-accusatory. Don't try it while emotionally flooded; cool down first. Say all three steps out loud and in order. Thinking steps 1 and 2 silently and only asking "why?" is not full perception checking. Consider cultural rules, timing and setting.

Example: "You didn't say anything at dinner (describe). I wasn't sure if you're tired from work or upset about something I said (two interpretations). What's going on? (ask)"

Section 3.1

Understanding Small Groups

Small group communication is interaction among three or more people connected through a common purpose, mutual influence and a shared identity. Interpersonal relationships focus on building relationships; small groups usually focus on completing a task or reaching a goal.

Four characteristics of small groups

Size3 minimum

Two people are a dyad, so a group needs at least three. There's no set upper limit, but past 15 to 20 members it's hard to call it small. The ideal size is the smallest number needed to do the task. Connections grow fast: 6 people have 15 possible pairs; 12 people have 66.

Structureinternal + external

Shaped by internal influences (member traits, such as an informed or motivated member emerging as leader) and external factors (size, task, resources). Groups also have formal networks (defined roles, hierarchy) and informal networks (unofficial connections inside and outside the group).

Interdependenceshared fate

Members share a common purpose and a common fate. One member's actions affect everyone. This is why students dislike group grades, but it can also raise quality when members are accountable.

Shared identityus vs. them

Shown through mission statements, names, slogans, symbols and shared stories. It sets the in-group apart from the out-group. Cohesion (liking the task and each other) builds trust, productivity and satisfaction.

Circle vs. wheel

Reachability is how one member is or isn't connected to the other members.

Tell them apart

Task and relational orientation are two ends of a continuum, not either/or. Every group has some of both. A family plans tasks but is mainly relational.

Three kinds of group tasks

  • Production: make something tangible (a report, a playground design, a fundraiser).
  • Discussion: talk something through without a right or wrong answer (a book club, a support group).
  • Problem-solving: devise a course of action to meet a specific need. The goal is a well-thought-out idea.

Secondary groups by reason for forming

  • Shared interest: social and leisure groups (film buffs, bird watchers).
  • Individual needs: study groups and support groups; usually discussion-oriented.
  • Service groups: meet others' needs but are task-oriented (Habitat for Humanity, Rotary Club).
  • Advocacy: form around a shared need (Gay Men's Health Crisis, a neighborhood stop-sign campaign).

Seven traits of successful teams

  • Clear and inspiring shared goals
  • A results-driven structure
  • Competent team members
  • A collaborative climate
  • High standards for performance
  • External support and recognition
  • Ethical and accountable leadership

Advantages and disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Shared decision makingOne expert can do better than a group
Shared resourcesSlower decisions
SynergyGroup polarization

Synergy in numbers

Compromise is 1+1=1.5. Cooperation is 1+1=2. Synergy is 1+1=3 or more, like two beams together holding more than twice what one holds.

When individuals beat groups

  • Groups win on tasks needing a broad range of information or skills, and non-expert groups often beat an individual expert because of error checking.
  • Individuals do better when the group is made of non-expert social loafers competing with an expert, or when the group has low expectations, is too large, the task is simple, or time is severely limited.
  • Under time pressure, one "point person" who coordinates and gives final approval works best.

Group polarization

A group decision ends up more extreme (riskier or more cautious) than what members would choose alone. It doesn't mean more disagreement or opposing sides. Causes: social comparison, persuasive arguments, and reduced individual accountability. Groups take more risks when they're large, members don't know each other well, and members are well-informed about the problem.

Section 3.4

Self-Disclosure and Interpersonal Communication

Self-disclosure is purposeful disclosure of personal information to another person. Everything we do reveals something, but it only counts as self-disclosure when it's on purpose. Wearing your team's cap specifically to show a new friend your loyalty counts. Disclosure doesn't have to be deep: superficial disclosure like small talk (your major, your hometown) starts relationships.

Three theories of self-disclosure

Social penetration theory

As we get to know someone, we disclose to each other reciprocally, and the disclosure changes in breadth (range of topics) and depth (how personal or sensitive). Like peeling an onion, we usually go through the layers gradually.

Relationships also balance needs in tension, called a dialectic, like walking a tightrope. The key one here is openness vs. closedness: we want to open up, but we also want privacy.

Social comparison theory

We evaluate ourselves by comparing ourselves with others. We disclose to find out:

  • Abilities and talents: are we superior or inferior? A positive reaction gives self-validation.
  • Beliefs and values: are they the same or different?
  • Fantasies or thoughts: are they acceptable or unacceptable?

This helps both the discloser and the receiver decide whether to pursue a relationship.

The Johari window

Named for creators Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham. It shows which parts of ourselves are open, hidden, blind and unknown.

Four categories of what we disclose

Observationswhat you did or experienced

"I live in a farmhouse in Illinois."

Thoughtsadds a judgment

"I think moving to the country was a good decision."

"I'm happy to wake up and look out at the corn fields. I feel lucky."

"I really want my best friend to move here too."

We usually start with observations and thoughts, then move to feelings and needs as the relationship grows. Exceptions: we disclose more deeply in a crisis, and more to a stranger we don't expect to see again.

Some people are naturally more transparent and others more opaque. Research links watching reality TV to more self-disclosure through blogging and video sharing.

When and how to disclose

  • Early in a conversation: leaves time to discuss, and you won't lose your nerve.
  • In the middle: lets you feel out the other person's mood and set the tone.
  • Late: limits your exposure to a negative reaction, but leaves no time to talk it through.
  • Unplanned: a direct question or someone else's disclosure can push you to reciprocate before you've thought about the risks. If you don't want to reciprocate, you can still support them by listening.
  • Face-to-face: feels more genuine and allows immediate feedback (a hug, follow-up questions), but you face the reaction right away.
  • Mediated (email, text, letter, call): more control over wording and distance from a negative reaction, but can feel less personal and loses nonverbal cues.

Effects on the relationship

Self-disclosure is circular: someone discloses, the receiver reacts, and the discloser processes the reaction. The receiver's attribution of why it happened shapes the reaction.

AttributionReceiver thinks the cause is…Coworker example
DispositionalThe discloser's personality"She's outgoing" or "she's fishing for information"
SituationalThe context or surroundings"She told me because we went to lunch"
InterpersonalThe relationship between them"She told me because we're best friends at work"
Likely test point

Interpersonal attributions strengthen intimacy and closeness most, because the receiver feels specially chosen. A dispositional attribution to a positive trait also makes the reaction more positive.

Secrets and outcomes

  • Receiving a secret makes you a co-owner of it, and you may feel the need to unburden yourself. Telling someone outside the discloser's social network can keep it secure.
  • In one study, 77 percent of office workers told not to share a disclosure told at least two people by the end of the day, for attention or to show power or connection.
  • When disclosure goes well: more relational intimacy and self-worth, less stress, more social support. It can even affect physical health; grieving spouses who didn't talk to friends had more health problems.
